# Company patterns worth testing

These are documented practices, not proof that a company’s overall structure is optimal. The translations below are design hypotheses for agent teams; human organizations have incentives, expertise, relationships, and legal accountability that software agents do not. Sources were accessed 2026-10-04.

| Company and documented practice | Useful mechanism | Agent translation | Tradeoff and limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Amazon — small teams and decision reversibility.** In an [Amazon interview with operations leader Jeff Wilke](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/workplace/avoiding-the-perils-of-scrappy), he describes funding one or two people to begin a “two-pizza team” after a product proposal, to avoid launching with an unorganized large group. CEO Andy Jassy’s [2024 shareholder letter](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/amazon-ceo-andy-jassy-2024-letter-to-shareholders) says reversible two-way-door decisions can be made quickly and locally, while difficult-to-reverse one-way-door decisions get more deliberate treatment. These are company accounts, not evidence that every Amazon team uses an identical structure today. | Small, focused teams own bounded outcomes; decision reversibility changes the required deliberation. | Bound each agent task with a clear owner and limited interfaces. Classify decisions by reversibility: let an agent act quickly within scope when rollback is cheap; require evidence, review, or human approval for irreversible changes. | Team size is a heuristic, not an optimal number. Small units can create silos or duplicate work; door classification can be disputed. Require agents to state rollback cost and blast radius, then test whether the gate prevents harm without slowing routine work. |
| **GitLab — DRI and public decision procedure.** Its current [handbook on decision-making](https://handbook.gitlab.com/handbook/leadership/making-decisions/) assigns a Directly Responsible Individual to lead information gathering and make the decision after seeking relevant input. It recommends a short proposal that defines scope, people consulted, alternatives, decision, and rationale; consultation does not mean consensus. | Separate “who gathers input” from “who decides,” and make the choice traceable. One named owner closes a decision so unresolved discussion does not become hidden veto. | For each issue or workstream, name one decision owner. Store a compact decision record in the repo with scope, consulted roles, alternatives, chosen option, rationale, and date. The coordinator can integrate without owning every domain decision. | A DRI can overrule useful dissent or make a poorly informed call. Require consultation for affected domains, record dissent and uncertainty, and offer escalation for policy, safety, or cross-boundary disputes. The handbook documents GitLab’s intended process; it does not establish uniform practice or outcomes. |
| **Toyota — jidoka, andon, and kaizen.** Toyota’s [Production System overview](https://global.toyota/en/company/plant-tours/production-system/) describes jidoka: stop when an abnormality appears; an andon call alerts a leader, and work resumes after resolution. Toyota’s [company account](https://global.toyota/en/company/vision-and-philosophy/production-system/) also describes daily incremental kaizen. | Give the person closest to a defect a way to surface it; make the stop signal visible; resolve the cause before proceeding; feed recurring defects into process improvement. | Any agent may report a failed check and pause its own dependent work. The coordinator blocks the affected transition, preserves evidence, assigns an incident lead if needed, and records cause and corrective action. Use repeated failures to update checks and guidance. | Over-triggered stops interrupt useful work; weak thresholds normalize false alarms. Define stop conditions and who may resume. Manufacturing line-stop practices do not prove the same thresholds suit software or research tasks. |
| **Valve — self-selection in a historical handbook.** Valve’s [official publications page](https://www.valvesoftware.com/en/publications) still offers its *Handbook for New Employees*, whose first edition is dated 2012 and describes a flat organization, peer project selection, and choosing work by moving one’s desk. This is evidence of a published 2012 self-description, not a verified account of Valve’s current structure. | Local initiative and peer choice can reveal motivation and expertise without a manager assigning every task. | Let agents propose or accept well-scoped tasks from a shared backlog when capability and dependencies permit. Keep an explicit integration owner and record ownership changes. | Self-selection can leave unattractive but necessary work undone, create coordination gaps, and reward visible work. Use a maintainer to cover neglected responsibilities and decide contested assignments; autonomy alone is not accountability. |

The most portable combination is small bounded work units (Amazon), one explicit decision owner with recorded consultation (GitLab), and a visible stop-and-learn path for defects (Toyota). Borrow Valve’s self-selection only for optional work with clear ownership. Keep mission, permissions, high-impact changes, and return-to-service authority explicit in the shared charter. Evaluate the combined workflow against a single-agent baseline; these company examples suggest mechanisms to test, not expected gains.
